Where the forest goes next.

Using only what satellites had recorded by the end of 2015, the model ranks every patch of standing forest by how likely it is to be cleared in the next eight years. Then we check it against what actually happened, on a stretch of map it never saw.

What the model leans on

Method

  1. Data. Hansen/UMD/Google/USGS/NASA Global Forest Change v1.11: tree cover in 2000 and the year of tree-cover loss, from Landsat at 30 m. One 1 by 0.75 degree window, cut into 150 m cells.
  2. Look-back only. Features use loss up to 2015: distance to the nearest cleared land, the share of cleared land and of forest at 450 m to 9 km, how much of that clearing is recent (2013 to 2015), and canopy cover. No coordinates.
  3. Label. A forest cell (at least half tree cover at the end of 2015) is positive if any of it is lost in 2016 to 2023.
  4. Split by place. Train on the west two thirds, test on the east third, with a gap of 8 cells so the two sides share no neighbourhood.
  5. Model. Gradient-boosted trees (scikit-learn). Compared with a one-feature baseline (distance to cleared land) and with plain nearby-clearing density.

Caveats. Tree-cover loss includes fire, logging and plantation harvest, not only conversion to farmland. The east of the window was cleared about twice as often as the west, so the test is harder than a random split, and swapping the roles (train east, test west) scores lower, which is reported above. The map is one region; results elsewhere will differ. Data licence: CC BY 4.0, Hansen et al., Science 342 (2013).
